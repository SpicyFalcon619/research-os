# **Overview**

## **Problems:**

Researchers typically have to **spend hundreds of hours** per year reading papers. Reading efficiently is **rarely taught.** Most early graduate students have to **learn on their own** using trial and error. Therefore, they **waste much effort** in the process and are frequently **frustrated**.

## **First Pass:**

### **Steps:**

1.  Read **title, abstract, intro**
2.  Read **section** & **sub-section** headings
3.  Read **Conclusion**
4.  Glance over **reference**, tick off ones I’ve already read

The first pass should take about **5-10 minutes**.

### **Answer 5 C’s:**

1.  **C**ategory: what type of paper is it? A measurement study, an analysis of an existing system, or a research prototype?
2.  **C**ontext: which other papers is it related to, and what theory does it build on?
3.  **C**orrectness: do the assumptions appear valid?
4.  **C**ontributions: what does the paper claim as its main contributions?
5.  **C**larity: is it well written?

### **When to choose NOT to read:**

-  Doesn’t **interest** me
-  I **don’t know enough** about the area to understand
-  The authors make **invalid** assumptions

The first pass is **adequate** for papers that are **not in my area** but may someday be **relevant** (so I can stop there).

### **Things to look out for when writing a paper:**

-  Most people make only **one** pass
-  Choose coherent **section** and **sub-section** titles
-  Write concise and comprehensive **abstracts**
-  Can be **rejected** if cannot be understood after the gist of one pass
-  Can be **never read** if the highlights of the paper is not understood after **5 minutes**

## **Second Pass:**

### **Steps:**

1.  Look carefully at the **figures, diagrams** and other **illustrations**. Specially **graph (axes properly labeled? results shown with error bars so the conclusions are statistically significant?)**. This is to separate **rushed, shoddy work** from the **truly excellent ones**
2.  Mark **relevant unread references** for further reading (good way to learn more about the **background**)

Second pass should take up to **an hour**. I should be able to **summarize** the **main thrust of the paper** with supporting evidence. This level of details is enough for a paper i am **interested in** but **not in my research speciality**.

### Sometimes **won’t understand** even after 2nd pass. **Reasons:**

1.  Subject matter is **new** with **unfamiliar terms** and **acronyms**
2.  Author may use a **proof** or **experimental technique** that **I don’t understand**
3.  Maybe poorly written with **unsubstantiated assertions** and many **forward references**
4.  Or maybe I’m just **tired** being up late :(

### **Now it’s time to choose:**

1.  Set the paper aside if **I don’t need it**
2.  Return later after reading **background materials**
3.  **Persevere** and go to the 3rd pass.

## **Third Pass:**

Required to **fully understand** a paper. Particularly as a **reviewer**.

### **Steps:**

-  Attempt to **virtually re-implement** the paper:
**1.** Make the **same assumptions** as the authors
**2. Re-create** the work
-  **Compare** the re-creation with the actual paper to easily identify a paper’s **innovations**, as well as its **hidden failings** and **assumptions**
-  Great **attention to detail** is required:
**1. Challenge** every assumption in every statement
**2.** Think about **how I would present** a particular idea
**3.** This **comparison** of actual with virtual can give a **sharp insight** into the proof and presentation technique in the paper
**4.** Can add this to my **collection of tools**
-  Should **note down ideas** for the future

Can take about **4-5 hours** for **beginners** and about **an hour** for an **experienced**.

### **After 3rd pass:**

-  Should be able to **reconstruct** the **entire structure** from **memory**
-  Identify its **strong** and **weak** points
-  Should be able to **pinpoint implicit assumptions, missing citations** to relevant work, and **potential issues** with **experimental** or **analytical techniques**

## **Literature Survey: Paper reading skills** are Put to the test. Requires me to **read tens of papers**, maybe in an **unfamiliar field**.

### **Steps:**

1.  Use an **academic search** engine like **Google Scholar** or **CiteSeer** and some **well-chosen keywords** to find **3-5 recent papers** in the area. Do **one pass on each paper** to get a **sense of the work** and then read their **related work** sections. This will give a **thumbnail summary** of the recent work and if I’m **lucky**, I’ll find a **pointer to a recent survey paper**. If I can find such a survey, I’m **done here** and then I should **read the survey**.
2.  **Otherwise**, find **shared citations** and **repeated author names** in the **bibliography**. Those are the **key papers** and **researchers** in that area. **Download** the key papers and **set them aside**. Go to the **websites** of the **key researchers** and see where they’ve published **recently**. This will help **identify** the **top conferences** in that field as the **best researchers** usually publish in the **top conferences**.
3.  Go to the **websites** for these **top conferences** and look through their **recent proceedings**. Quick scan can identify recent **high-quality related work**. Those papers along with the ones **set aside earlier** will constitute the **first version** of the survey. Make **2 passes** through those papers. If they all cite a key paper that was **not found earlier, obtain and read it, iterating as necessary**.

**My own caution (not from Keshav):** shared citations and repeated author names are clues to key papers, not proof. Papers can be cited a lot because they are popular, authors often cite their own group, and the same name can be different people. I should still check the paper itself.
